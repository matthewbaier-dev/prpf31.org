PRPF31 Explained

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited conditions in which the retina's light-sensing cells lose function over time. PRPF31 is one of the genes that cause it. RP caused by PRPF31 is also called RP11, its older name. Genetic test reports usually name the gene and the specific change in it that causes the disease, also called a mutation.

Every cell edits its genetic instructions before using them, a step called splicing. The PRPF31 protein is one of the parts that hold the cell's editing machinery together. Most of us with PRPF31 RP have one copy of the gene that doesn't work, so our cells make less of the protein than they need. The leading explanation for why the retina suffers is that it is one of the busiest tissues in the body. The rest of the body does not seem to be affected.

PRPF31 RP is rare. Even so, PRPF31 is one of the most common genetic causes of autosomal dominant RP and is probably undercounted, because some people with a PRPF31 change never have symptoms.

Symptoms

  • Reduced Night Vision. This is usually the first sign. Trouble seeing in dim light or moving around in the dark often starts in childhood or the teenage years. In children it can look like clumsiness at night.
  • Narrowing Side Vision. Over the years that follow, the field of vision closes in from the edges, which is sometimes called tunnel vision.
  • Central Vision. This tends to last longest. Some people keep good reading vision until a late stage.
  • Other Changes. Some people also notice sensitivity to glare or changes in color vision.

The pace varies widely, even within one family, and when symptoms start can depend partly on the type of PRPF31 change.

RP can also bring cataracts and swelling in the retina. Both can be treated, which is one reason regular check-ups matter.

Inheritance

Everyone has two copies of the PRPF31 gene, one inherited from each biological parent. PRPF31 RP is autosomal dominant: one changed copy of the gene (a mutation) is enough to put someone at risk. Each child of a person who has it has a 50% chance of inheriting it, sons and daughters alike.

What makes PRPF31 unusual is that some people with a PRPF31 change never lose any sight. This is called incomplete penetrance. In some people the healthy copy of the gene works harder and makes up most of the shortfall. Inherited differences act like a dimmer on the healthy copy and help decide who is affected. The best confirmed is a stretch of repeated DNA right next to the gene called MSR1: four copies of the repeat on the healthy copy, instead of three, are linked to staying symptom-free. Another is a gene called CNOT3, which can act as a brake on PRPF31. Researchers are still working out how these and other factors combine. So the condition can seem to skip a generation: a parent with normal sight can still pass it on.

Occasionally a PRPF31 change appears for the first time in someone with no family history. A genetic counselor can help a family work through who should be tested and when.

Genetic Testing

A genetic test turns a diagnosis of "RP" into "PRPF31 RP." It confirms the cause, helps relatives understand their own risk and is usually needed to take part in clinical trials.

In the US, the Foundation Fighting Blindness offers no-cost genetic testing and counseling for people with a clinical diagnosis of an inherited retinal disease, ordered by any eye care professional.

Some PRPF31 changes are large deletions or sit deep inside the gene. Not every test looks for them. When dominant RP runs in a family and no gene has been found, a genetic counselor can advise whether further testing makes sense.

In Canada, provincial health plans cover genetic testing for inherited retinal diseases when it is judged appropriate. Fighting Blindness Canada explains how to get tested in each province and runs a patient registry for Canadians.

Elsewhere, Retina International can point to patient organizations and testing in other countries.

Our Advantage

One treatment for all

Most inherited eye diseases need a treatment built for the specific gene and sometimes for the specific change in it. PRPF31 is different. Almost every PRPF31 change ends the same way: our cells make too little PRPF31 protein. So a treatment that raises the protein could help nearly all of us, whatever our exact change.

The same idea, turning up the healthy copy of a gene, is being tested in other conditions caused by too little of a protein, including a severe childhood epilepsy and an inherited optic nerve disease. Progress in any of them strengthens the case for all of them.

Research

There is currently no approved treatment for PRPF31 RP.

Research is active. Because most of us make too little PRPF31 protein, most work aims to raise the amount, by one of three routes:

  • Turn Up the Healthy Copy

    Repeated injections into the eye help the working copy make more protein. This approach is in clinical trials.

  • Add a Working Copy

    A harmless virus delivers a fresh copy of the gene to the retina, ideally in a single treatment. Lab studies in patients' own retinal cells have shown this can repair the cells' editing and their cleanup work. It is in preclinical development.

  • Repair the Faulty Copy

    Short strands of genetic material correct how the faulty copy is read. This fits only certain specific changes. Researchers have shown it partly correcting one such change in patient cells.

All of these approaches are investigational. None has yet been proven safe and effective for PRPF31 RP.

Which Trials Fit You

It depends on how much vision you still have.

  • Earlier Stage. The three routes above aim to slow or stop vision loss, so they need light-sensing cells that are still alive. They are aimed at people who still have useful vision. Some trials of approaches that work regardless of the gene also enroll people at this stage, PRPF31 included.
  • Advanced Stage. Optogenetics, which makes surviving retinal cells sensitive to light, aims to restore some sight after most light-sensing cells are gone. It works regardless of the gene involved, but its trials have enrolled only people with severe vision loss. Most of us who still have good central vision would not be eligible. Some cell-based therapies are also aimed at this stage.

We don't endorse any company, treatment or trial. To see which clinical trials are recruiting, search ClinicalTrials.gov for PRPF31.

Why We Organize

As treatments for PRPF31 move toward clinical trials, a connected community can help them reach us sooner.

  • Finding Participants. Finding enough eligible people is one of the most common reasons clinical trials are delayed. In a rare condition like ours, every person who can be found matters.
  • Beyond Registries. Patient registries are valuable and we encourage everyone to join one. But they include only people who have signed up. Researchers usually see coded records rather than names, so there is no way to start a two-way conversation. A community where we know each other can share what taking part in a trial is like and talk through questions and concerns together.
  • Data for Our Gene. When regulators review a treatment, they look closely at what a trial measured and whether it matters to patients. Results from people with PRPF31, and a clear voice on what matters most to us, help make the case for treatments built for our gene.
  • Focus. Broad efforts serve many conditions at once. By focusing on one gene and pooling our attention and resources, we can help move research toward treatment faster.

Questions

What Is RP11?

RP11 is the older name for retinitis pigmentosa caused by changes in the PRPF31 gene. PRPF31 RP and RP11 are the same condition.

Does PRPF31 Affect Anything Besides the Eyes?

It doesn't seem to. The gene is active throughout the body, but PRPF31 RP appears to affect only the retina.

Where Should I Go If I Think I Am Affected?

Start with an eye doctor and ask for a referral to a retina specialist who sees inherited retinal diseases. The specialist can confirm the diagnosis and arrange genetic testing. In the US, the Foundation Fighting Blindness keeps a list of these specialists and offers free testing and counseling.

Do I Need to See a Retina Specialist?

Yes. A retina specialist who sees inherited retinal diseases can track how your vision changes over time and tell you about clinical trials that may fit. Regular check-ups also catch treatable problems such as cataracts and swelling in the retina.

Do I Need to See a Genetic Counselor?

It is a good idea if you have PRPF31 RP or might have a PRPF31 change. A genetic counselor can explain your test result and what it means for your relatives, including those with normal sight. They can also talk through testing for family members and options for planning a family. In the US, the no-cost testing program includes counseling. To find a genetic counselor in the US or Canada, use the National Society of Genetic Counselors' Find a Genetic Counselor directory.

What Should I Do After a PRPF31 Diagnosis?

Keep up regular check-ups with a retina specialist and talk with a genetic counselor about what the result means for your family. Consider joining the free My Retina Tracker Registry so researchers and clinical trials can find you. Our Next Steps cover each of these. Our private group is a place to talk with others who have been through it.

Will I Definitely Lose My Sight?

Not necessarily. Some people with a PRPF31 change never develop symptoms. For those who do, the pace varies widely, even within one family. Central vision tends to last longest. Some people keep good reading vision until a late stage.

How Fast Does It Progress?

It varies widely, even within one family, and some people with a PRPF31 change never develop symptoms. A retina specialist can track how each person's vision changes over time.

Can I Do Anything Now to Stop It?

Nothing has yet been proven to stop or slow PRPF31 RP. Regular check-ups still matter, because cataracts and swelling in the retina can be treated. Genetic testing and the registry make it easier to be considered for clinical trials. Ask your retina specialist before taking supplements such as high-dose vitamin A, which can carry risks.

Can My Children Inherit It Even If I Have Normal Vision?

Yes, if you have a PRPF31 change. Even if you have no symptoms, each of your children has a 50% chance of inheriting it. A child who inherits it may or may not develop symptoms. For family members or children at risk who have no symptoms, an eye exam is a good place to start. When to have genetic testing is a personal decision. A genetic counselor can explain the options for testing family members.

Can PRPF31 Skip a Generation?

It can seem to. The change itself can't skip a generation, but the symptoms can. Some people with a PRPF31 change never lose any sight, but they can still pass it on. So PRPF31 RP may show up in a grandparent and a grandchild but not the parent in between. If someone tests negative for the change found in their family, they are not at risk for PRPF31 RP and cannot pass it on to their children.

Is There a Treatment?

Not yet. No treatment is approved, but several are in development.

Are There Clinical Trials for PRPF31?

Yes. Search ClinicalTrials.gov for PRPF31 to see what is recruiting. Most trials require a genetic test that confirms PRPF31. Which trials fit you also depends on how much vision you still have.

About Us

PRPF31.org is the patient community for PRPF31 retinitis pigmentosa. It was started in 2026 by people living with PRPF31 for people with PRPF31 changes and their families, wherever they live. It helps our community speak with researchers, doctors and foundations. We welcome links and partnerships with other patient organizations.

It is independent and self-funded: it doesn't sell anything, takes no money from companies and doesn't endorse any company, treatment or trial.
